The Fallacy of ‘Under-Promise & Over-Deliver’  
AKA: Shoot Yourself in the Foot & Wander Around a Salt Mine  
By Vasco Ferreira Pinto (Analyst at OmniTek Consulting)  

Business aphorisms are ubiquitous, fun (for me at least) and generally harmless, even to wide-eyed analysts like myself. Harmless of course, until you believe them.  

**Under-Promise & Over-Deliver (UPOD)** makes just enough sense that you might believe it, but hopefully by the end of this quick read its flaws will be all over the kitchen floor (or wherever you’re reading this).  

**Defense:**  
UPOD has a good idea behind it—an idea that is known to be true. People/Clients hate being disappointed. Disappointment is a two-sided game set by expectations and results.  

Usually:  
**Expectations > Results = Disappointment**  
**Expectations < Results = No Disappointment**  

However, expectations have a *surprisingly* bigger role than results. A good movie can get ruined by the expectation that it was going to be better than *‘The Shawshank Redemption’*. A pinch to your arm can be great if you were expecting a punch to the face. So summarily, expectations have a bigger role than results. Results, though, are not valueless and can reach critical levels at which they determine satisfaction: *‘Whiplash’* is a great movie even if it isn’t *Shawshank*, and getting kicked in the shins is bad even if you expected a whooping.  

**Anchoring Bias & the UPOD Fallacy**  
Daniel Kahneman, a Nobel Prize winner in Economics and master of the brain’s inefficiencies, identifies a cognitive bias called *anchoring* in *Thinking Fast and Slow*. As Kahneman explains:  
> “The anchoring effect is a cognitive bias where people rely too heavily on the first piece of information they receive (the ‘anchor’) when making decisions.”  

This bias suggests that under-promising could theoretically lead to higher satisfaction by setting lower expectations. However, the *study* referenced in this article challenges this premise, arguing that *“there is no margin in over-delivery.”* While customers may appreciate exceeding expectations, the *effort* required to over-deliver yields *less satisfaction* than simply meeting promises.  

**Numeric Example:**  
Assume:  
- Rate of return for delivering = 100%  
- Rate of return for over-delivering = 50%  

When under-promising:  
- Promise: 8  
- Deliver: 10  
- Total Rate of Return = (8 * 100%) + (2 * 50%) = 9  

When promising accurately:  
- Promise: 10  
- Deliver: 10  
- Total Rate of Return = (10 * 100%) = 10  

This demonstrates that *accurate promises yield higher returns* than under-promising.  

**Offense: The Flaws of UPOD**  
Under-promising is not just flawed—it’s a risky strategy for three main reasons:  

1. **Competitive Disadvantage:** In scenarios where alternatives exist, under-promising risks being outpitched by competitors who promise what they can deliver.  
2. **Expectation Adjustment:** Clients often adjust upward when they recognize an under-promise, leading to disappointment. As Kahneman’s anchoring theory suggests, this effect weakens as clients gain knowledge, making under-promising *stale* in today’s informed B2B landscape.  
3. **Efficiency Loss:** By definition, under-promising leads to over-delivery, which, as shown above, yields *lower returns* than simply meeting promises.  

**The Reality of UPOD**  
The vision of UPOD is enticing: *“You humbly promise something you can definitely deliver, then work your sox off and deliver something that leaves the client flabbergasted.”* However, the reality is less rosy. Competitors like Jennie, who promise what they can deliver, avoid the risks of under-promising and achieve higher returns by focusing on *accurate delivery* rather than over-delivering.  

**Conclusion**  
The economics of UPOD are irrational. In an era where clients are knowledgeable and information is ubiquitous, under-promising risks backfiring. Best case: inefficiency. Worst case: losing business.
