The exascale computing era is here, marked by the delivery of the U.S. Department of Energy’s (DOE’s) first exascale system, **Frontier**, in 2022. This milestone, alongside upcoming systems like **Aurora** and **El Capitan**, represents a paradigm shift in computational power, enabling simulations of complex physical phenomena at unprecedented scales. As **Andrew Siegel**, senior scientist at Argonne National Laboratory and ECP director for Applications Development, explains, “ECP emphasizes the commonalities between each of the focus areas and provides an environment where we can identify with each other, share experiences and ideas, and understand one another while still being unique in our abilities.” This collaborative framework has been pivotal in maximizing the benefits of high-performance computing (HPC) for national security and economic competitiveness.

The **Exascale Computing Project (ECP)**, a seven-year, $1.8 billion initiative, has fostered a unified ecosystem of application developers, software technologists, and hardware vendors. This model, described by **Mike Heroux**, senior scientist at Sandia National Laboratories and ECP director for Software Technology, as “unique in that everyone involved in the project has the same mission,” has enabled breakthroughs in performance portability and cross-domain collaboration. For instance, the **Kokkos** and **RAJA** software frameworks have revolutionized exascale development by enabling hardware-agnostic code, allowing developers to write once and run across GPUs from multiple vendors. As Siegel notes, “Kokkos and RAJA are examples of higher level, more application friendly ways of programming for GPUs that represent an intermediate layer between the application and the native programming languages of the platform.”

A key challenge in exascale computing has been adapting codes to heterogeneous architectures—systems combining GPUs and CPUs. **Doug Kothe**, former ECP Director, highlights the necessity of “innovations in data structures, algorithms, and software development methodologies that perform well independently of specific accelerator features.” This has led to the creation of performance-portable programming models, exemplified by the **AMReX** co-design project, which supports adaptive mesh refinement for exascale simulations in fields like astrophysics and combustion. **Erik Draeger**, deputy director for ECP Applications Development, likens the ECP model to “frontier homesteading,” emphasizing how the project transformed isolated efforts into a collaborative “computing symphony” where specialists in applications, software, and hardware co-create solutions.

The success of ECP is underscored by its impact on real-world challenges. The **EQSIM** earthquake simulation application, for example, leveraged **RAJA** to run physics-based simulations on **Frontier**, achieving performance targets far beyond expectations. As Draeger explains, “The EQSIM team iterated back and forth with the RAJA team and worked with them to evaluate what worked well and what didn’t, and the combination was a massive success.” Similarly, the **EXAALT** application demonstrated a 500x speedup on exascale systems, showcasing the power of integrated teams.

Looking ahead, **Lori Diachin**, ECP Director, asserts that the project has “provided the foundation for the next generation of computational science breakthroughs.” The lessons from ECP—such as the “shared fate” philosophy and co-design collaboration—will shape the future of HPC, enabling exascale systems to drive advancements in energy, materials science, artificial intelligence, and healthcare. As Draeger concludes, “With every generation of machine, it becomes much harder for a domain specialist to be an expert in everything needed to do both the science on the machine and get the applications to run efficiently… ECP’s shared fate paradigm has promoted unity between the different DOE laboratories, academia, and industry and has pushed computing innovation to new heights.”

The ECP model exemplifies how a unified ecosystem can unlock the full potential of exascale computing, ensuring that the next generation of scientific discoveries is not just imagined but realized.
