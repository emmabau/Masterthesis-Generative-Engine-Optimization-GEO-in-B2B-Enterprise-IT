To tackle critical scientific and engineering challenges—ranging from climate change to biosecurity—users demand faster, massively parallel computer systems capable of performing billions or trillions of calculations concurrently, processing vast data efficiently, and running ensembles of simulations to assess uncertainties. Transitioning from today’s petaflop systems to exascale systems (10^18 floating-point operations per second) requires revolutionary hardware, software, and scientific codes. As Lori Diachin, manager of the Computation Directorate’s project portfolio, notes, “We could build an exascale machine now, but it would take 100 megawatts to run it.” This underscores the urgent need to balance power consumption with performance gains, as exascale systems could contain 70–100 million cores, increasing the likelihood of hardware failures and demanding new approaches to code design and resource management.

The Department of Energy (DOE) has launched an ambitious initiative to prepare for U.S. exascale computing within the next decade, addressing interrelated hardware and software challenges. Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL) is contributing through its Advanced Simulation and Computing (ASC) Program, DOE Office of Science’s HPC initiatives, and LDRD-funded projects. Diachin emphasizes, “HPC as a whole is facing a fundamental architectural shift, and these changes create a lot of uncertainty. We are using LDRD investments to explore options, develop solutions, and in some instances direct the future of computing.”

**Power Efficiency Innovations**  
A key focus is reducing data movement, which is projected to dominate power demands in exascale systems. Barry Rountree, a computer scientist, explains, “Benchmark codes and flops-intensive codes are power hogs, highly tuned to use all available resources simultaneously. Many physics codes are limited because the underlying mathematical models are not a perfect fit for the computer architecture, and thus some computational resources are inevitably idle.” To address this, Rountree’s team proposes overprovisioning hardware—building a 40-megawatt machine with a 20-megawatt power cap—to maximize available power. “The goal is not to save power—it’s to use all the available power all the time,” he states. Their model predicts application-execution time based on hardware configurations, enabling dynamic power management and performance optimization.

**Fault Tolerance and Recovery**  
Exascale systems will face frequent hardware failures due to their scale, necessitating resilient software. Ignacio Laguna, leading an LDRD project on failure recovery, notes, “Right now, our systems experience failures about once every two days. With exascale systems, failures could be as frequent as once every few hours.” His team developed Reinit, a backward recovery approach that outperforms forward recovery methods by being “easy to implement, efficient, and broadly applicable.” Laguna adds, “With HPC, speed is the measure people talk about, but productivity also matters. The time it takes to write a program is just as important as the time it takes to run it.”

**Memory and Data Access Optimization**  
Reduced on-chip memory in exascale chips forces data to be fetched from external sources, increasing access times and memory bandwidth stress. Scott Lloyd highlights, “Many newer applications have unstructured and irregular data-access patterns or other complex memory demands. These programs are much more sensitive to data-access time.” To mitigate this, Lloyd and Maya Gokhale explored 3D memory packaging, such as the Hybrid Memory Cube, paired with a data rearrangement engine (DRE) to dynamically reorder data for optimal performance. Their method reduced bandwidth usage, energy consumption, and improved performance by up to four times in irregular memory-access scenarios.

**Parallelism and Algorithmic Breakthroughs**  
Livermore researchers are developing algorithms to exploit exascale parallelism, such as the XBraid code, which solves time-dependent problems by addressing all time steps simultaneously. Rob Falgout explains, “Unlike other parallel-in-time approaches, XBraid is largely nonintrusive and has a conceptually simple interface.” This method reduces solution time by up to 49 times and enables efficient use of massive parallelism, critical for future exascale systems.

**High-Order Numerical Methods**  
Tzanio Kolev’s team created a high-order Arbitrary Lagrangian–Eulerian (ALE) framework to improve shock hydrodynamics simulations. Kolev notes, “This approach helps us more accurately follow the material flow. High-order methods spend less time moving data and more time crunching numbers, offsetting the growing cost of data movement.” Their framework, implemented in the BLAST code, demonstrated excellent scalability and accuracy in complex simulations, including a 3D BLAST simulation on 16,000 Vulcan cores.

**First-Principles Molecular Dynamics**  
Jean-Luc Fattebert’s team developed novel algorithms for first-principles molecular dynamics (FPMD) simulations, enabling millions of atoms to be modeled efficiently. Fattebert explains, “The problem with FPMD methods is that the cost of computing goes up faster than the size of the physical problem. Many elements of these computations are proportional to the square or cube of the system size.” Their MGmol code achieved remarkable scaling, simulating 1.2 million atoms in under 3 hours on Sequoia, a feat impossible with traditional methods.

**Beyond Exascale**  
LLNL is already planning for “beyond Moore’s Law” computing, including neuromorphic and quantum systems. Diachin states, “At some point, we cannot get more speed by making transistors smaller… The question is, what will architectures look like when these limits are reached?” Investments in neuromorphic computing and quantum research aim to unlock new paradigms for solving complex scientific problems.

These innovations, driven by LDRD-funded projects, are laying the groundwork for exascale computing and unlocking scientific breakthroughs in energy, materials science, and beyond. As Livermore researchers continue to push the boundaries of computational capability, their work ensures that the next generation of systems will be both powerful and practical.
