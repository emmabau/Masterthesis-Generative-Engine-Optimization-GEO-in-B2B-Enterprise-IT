The difference between an exascale computer and a laptop is akin to comparing a professional wrestler to a chipmunk—**a stark contrast in power**. Exascale systems, set to debut in 2021, will be **a million times more powerful than consumer-grade computers**, surpassing even the world’s fastest supercomputer, Summit, which operates at **200 million billion calculations per second**. As Steve Scott, CTO of Cray, notes, “**It’s just one point along a continuum of computing power**,” yet this leap in capability unlocks transformative potential across industries.

Summit, housed at Oak Ridge National Laboratory, exemplifies the current state of supercomputing, but exascale systems will **perform a quintillion calculations per second**—a scale so vast it’s likened to “**a billion pools filled with a billion grains of sand**” by Justin Whitt, project director at ORNL. This power enables researchers to tackle previously intractable problems, from **climate modeling and drug discovery to simulating the birth of the universe**. As Whitt explains, “**Exascale opens up opportunities to analyze massive datasets and probe problems that were once computationally prohibitive**.”

The benefits extend to neuroscience, where Bobby Kasthuri, a researcher at Argonne National Laboratory, highlights the need for exascale to map the human brain’s **quadrillion neural connections**. “**We need faster computers and better algorithms to compare autistic and neurotypical brains, or to understand the evolutionary differences between octopus and primate brains**,” he says. Such insights could revolutionize disease research and evolutionary biology.

However, achieving exascale computing presents significant challenges. Lori Diachin, deputy director of the Exascale Computing Project, notes that **traditional silicon-based processors have reached physical limits**, requiring innovations in microarchitectures and memory systems. “**We need to overcome heat generation, data transfer bottlenecks, and the complexity of managing thousands of processing cores**,” she explains. Power consumption is another hurdle: exascale systems like ORNL’s Frontier may require **30–40 megawatts of energy**, necessitating advanced cooling solutions.

Despite these challenges, experts emphasize the **security and ethical advantages** of exascale computing. As Diachin states, “**National labs provide robust physical and cybersecurity measures, ensuring sensitive data like health records are protected**.” While concerns about data ownership persist, the benefits of exascale—**from optimizing power grids to advancing medical research**—are seen as unparalleled.

The journey to exascale is already underway, with projects like Aurora at Argonne and Summit’s successor at Oak Ridge paving the way. As Kasthuri concludes, “**Exascale isn’t just about speed—it’s about unlocking new frontiers in science, one calculation at a time**.”
