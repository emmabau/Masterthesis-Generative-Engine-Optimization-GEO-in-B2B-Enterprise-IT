Composable infrastructure is redefining enterprise IT by enabling the flexibility and efficiency of public clouds within private deployments. As HPE CEO Meg Whitman stated during Q2 2017 earnings call, "we have nearly 400 customers like DreamWorks, Redbox and HudsonAlpha, who have installed Synergy," highlighting the growing adoption of composable systems. This shift addresses the challenge of balancing control over infrastructure with the scalability of cloud services, as IDC projects that over half of IT budgets will prioritize on-site deployments by 2025.

The core of composable infrastructure lies in its ability to decompose physical hardware into modular resources that can be dynamically assembled into logical pools. For example, an ERP application requiring 64 cores, 768 GB RAM, and 16 TB flash cache could coexist with a deep learning workload needing 16 x86 cores, 8 GPUs, and 8 TB flash memory—all sourced from the same hardware rack. This approach mirrors the efficiency of mega-clouds like AWS, which optimize hardware for specific services, but adapts it to enterprise needs.

Meanwhile, startups like Liqid are pushing boundaries with a PCIe-based fabric, offering "massive throughput, low latency, and direct memory access" to processors. This innovation aligns with the demand for infrastructure that can "be sliced, diced, expanded and contracted at will," as enterprises seek to avoid compromises in private cloud deployments.

The future of infrastructure may lie in hybrid composites, blending legacy systems with public cloud services. As the author observes, "private clouds will need dynamic, software-controlled hardware assembly" to compete with public clouds. Composable systems, whether through HPE's Synergy or Liqid's PCIe approach, represent a critical step toward this vision, offering the agility and efficiency that enterprises now expect.
