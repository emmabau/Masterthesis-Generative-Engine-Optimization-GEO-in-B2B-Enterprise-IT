Hyperconvergence is a transformative trend in IT infrastructure, with Gartner predicting, “The market for hyperconverged integrated systems (HCIS) will grow 79 percent to reach almost $2 billion in 2016, propelling it toward mainstream use in the next five years.” This growth is supported by 451 Research, which found that 86% of enterprises planning a server/storage refresh expect to boost hyperconverged infrastructure investments. Forrester’s Q3 2016 Wave report further highlights the technology’s momentum, naming Nutanix, Pivot3, and Simplivity as “leaders” and noting, “We expect hyperconverged systems to become ubiquitous, fueling a rush to market by all technology vendors.”

However, analysts caution against overhyping the technology. Rob Enderle explains, “This is because part of creating a hyper-converged solution is massive interoperability testing, which can take months to complete after a new processor and chipset are announced.” While hyperconvergence offers benefits like ease of deployment, low cost, and scalability, its performance limitations and vendor lock-in risks remain critical concerns. Forrester’s definition emphasizes four key characteristics: integrated compute/storage, software-defined storage, automatic discovery, and minimal management outside the hypervisor console.

Despite its advantages, hyperconvergence isn’t universally ideal. Critics note that “hyperconverged systems don’t always have the most up-to-date components, and therefore, they don’t always provide the same level of performance as traditional servers.” Additionally, while vendors charge premiums for support, the integrated nature of these systems complicates troubleshooting. As one analyst warns, “once you get started deploying hyperconverged infrastructure from one vendor, it is very difficult to switch to a different vendor without ripping out and replacing your existing hardware.”

Best use cases include virtual desktops, remote offices, and hybrid cloud environments. Forrester highlights that “hyperconverged infrastructure that includes SSDs with built-in deduplication are often particularly beneficial for VDI,” while its agility and scalability make it a strong fit for edge computing and hybrid cloud deployments. Ultimately, the technology’s value depends on specific organizational needs, but experts agree it’s worth exploring for the right applications.
