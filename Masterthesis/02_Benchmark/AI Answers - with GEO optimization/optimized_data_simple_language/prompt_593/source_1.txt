Exascale computing represents the next major leap in supercomputer technology, offering unprecedented speed to tackle global challenges like climate change, cancer research, and material science. Unlike quantum computers, which use a completely different approach, exascale systems are advanced versions of traditional computers with massively enhanced hardware.

To understand their power, consider how computers measure performance: floating point operations per second (FLOPS). A person can solve one addition problem in about one second, but computers are far faster. Modern PCs operate in the gigaflop range (150 billion FLOPS), while the 1996 DOE supercomputer ASCI Red hit 1.34 teraflops. Exascale systems, however, are unimaginably faster—capable of over 1 quintillion FLOPS (1 exaflop), more than a million times faster than ASCI Red.

Building these systems is a complex challenge. Early estimates suggested they would consume as much energy as 50 homes, but ongoing research has significantly reduced this. Scientists must also ensure reliability, manage vast data movement, and prevent bottlenecks in processing.

Why are exascale computers critical? They enable breakthroughs in climate modeling, nanoscale material design, fusion energy, and astrophysics. They also support national security by advancing nuclear deterrence research.

The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) leads exascale development through three key areas:
1. **Application Development**: Creating software to fully utilize exascale power.
2. **Software Technology**: Tools for managing data, integrating systems, and handling massive datasets.
3. **Hardware & Integration**: Partnering to build components, set standards, and train experts.

DOE is deploying the first U.S. exascale systems: Frontier (ORNL), Aurora (Argonne), and El Capitan (Lawrence Livermore). These systems are already powering groundbreaking research, such as advanced climate simulations and particle physics studies.

**Key Milestones**:
- 1940s: Computers began steadily increasing in performance.
- 1940s: Colossus (first electronic computer) operated at 500,000 FLOPS.
- 1964: CDC 6600 became the first supercomputer with 3 million FLOPS.
- 1985: Cray-2 reached over 1 billion FLOPS.
- 1996: ASCI Red achieved 1.34 trillion FLOPS (teraflops).
- 2008: Roadrunner became the first petaflop supercomputer.
