Register now to access the ACM-SIGHPC TechTalk, "Developing Vascular Digital Twins for Proactive Healthcare," presented by 2023 ACM Prize in Computing recipient Amanda Randles, Duke University, and moderated by ACM Distinguished Scientist Michela Taufer, University of Tennessee Knoxville. The session, hosted by Doug Kothe (Exascale Computing Project, ORNL) and John West (Texas Advanced Computing Center), explores the integration of high-performance computing (HPC) in biomedical modeling. The Exascale Computing Project (ECP), initiated in 2016 under the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE), focuses on creating a vertically integrated software stack for exascale systems (≥10¹⁸ FLOPS) to address grand challenges in science and engineering. This includes optimizing memory hierarchies, communication patterns, and heterogeneous architectures through compile-time and runtime techniques. The ECP, a joint effort between the DOE Office of Science and National Nuclear Security Administration, employs over 100 interdisciplinary teams to develop scalable algorithms and hardware-software co-design frameworks.

In a keynote by ACM Fellow Jack Dongarra (University of Tennessee), the evolution of HPC over the past decade is analyzed, emphasizing the need for adaptive numerical libraries and algorithms to manage dynamic, distributed, and parallel environments. Challenges include mitigating latency variability, optimizing memory bandwidth, and ensuring fault tolerance in exascale systems. The discussion highlights the role of runtime systems and compiler optimizations in enabling scalable applications across diverse architectures.

The webinar on HPC in aerospace engineering, presented by Brian E. Mitchell (GE Global Research) and moderated by Wilfred Pinfold, details the application of HPC in computational fluid dynamics (CFD) and finite element analysis (FEA) for jet engine and gas turbine design. GE’s approach emphasizes co-design of hardware/software ecosystems, leveraging exascale capabilities to accelerate physics-based simulations while balancing computational cost and energy efficiency. The session also explores the impact of emerging HPC hardware on predictive modeling and real-time optimization in industrial systems.

Salman Habib and Rajeev Thakur (Argonne National Laboratory) present the Hardware/Hybrid Accelerated Cosmology Code (HACC), a framework designed for exascale scalability. HACC employs hybrid CPU-GPU architectures and adaptive mesh refinement to achieve high performance on peta- and exa-scale systems. The framework’s modular design enables rapid deployment across heterogeneous platforms, with case studies demonstrating its application in cosmological simulations and large-scale data analysis.

Mark Ebersole (NVIDIA) discusses the transition from serial to parallel computing, emphasizing the role of GPUs in accelerating scientific workflows. The session covers CUDA’s thread hierarchy (blocks, grids), GPU-accelerated libraries (e.g., cuBLAS, cuDNN), and directive-based programming (OpenACC). Challenges include managing memory coalescing, thread divergence, and latency hiding in large-scale parallel applications. Future directions include heterogeneous computing and quantum-inspired algorithms for exascale systems.

Bill Gropp (University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign) addresses parallel execution models, focusing on the interplay between programming paradigms (e.g., MPI, OpenMP) and system architecture. The session critiques traditional approaches for scalability limitations and advocates for novel execution models that abstract hardware-specific details while enabling efficient resource utilization. Key topics include load balancing, communication-avoiding algorithms, and runtime systems for dynamic task scheduling.
