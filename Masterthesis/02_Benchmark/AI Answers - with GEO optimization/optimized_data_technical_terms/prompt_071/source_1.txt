Five Direct-Impact Benefits of Direct Liquid Cooling (DLC) in Data Centers  
Sanna Makinen  

Air cooling has long been the conventional method for thermal management in data centers, but its limitations are becoming critical as rack power densities exceed 50 kW per rack. Verne Finland is pioneering the integration of direct liquid cooling (DLC) into its Pori data center, a move driven by the exponential growth of AI workloads and high-performance computing (HPC) demands. Modern data centers face escalating thermal challenges, with air cooling systems struggling to maintain acceptable thermal gradients for high-density hardware.  

**The Growing Need for Liquid Cooling in Data Centers**  
The AI data center market is projected to reach $50 billion by 2027, driven by the proliferation of machine learning, edge computing, and exascale HPC. Traditional air cooling systems, which rely on convective heat transfer with air’s low thermal conductivity (~0.026 W/m·K), cannot dissipate the heat generated by modern processors efficiently. Liquid cooling, with its superior heat transfer coefficient (up to 1,000 times higher than air), offers a scalable solution. Pairing DLC with air cooling can reduce power usage effectiveness (PUE) to below 1.1, significantly lowering operational costs and carbon footprints.  

**How Direct Liquid Cooling Works**  
Direct liquid cooling employs cold plates with microchannel heat exchangers to directly absorb heat from IT components. Cold plates are thermally coupled to CPUs, GPUs, and other heat-generating hardware, transferring heat to a coolant (typically water or dielectric fluid) via conduction. The coolant is then circulated to a heat exchanger, where thermal energy is rejected to the environment or repurposed for district heating. This closed-loop system eliminates the need for air conditioning, reducing energy consumption by up to 40% compared to traditional air cooling.  

**Five Direct-Impact Benefits of DLC**  
1. **Energy Efficiency**  
   DLC reduces thermal resistance by leveraging liquid’s high specific heat capacity (4.18 kJ/kg·K) and thermal conductivity (up to 0.6 W/m·K for water). For example, Verne’s DLC3000 rack solution can cool up to 80 kW per rack, achieving PUE <1.1 and reducing cooling energy consumption by 35–45%. This is critical for data centers with power densities exceeding 20 kW/m², where air cooling becomes thermally inadequate.  

2. **Improved Performance**  
   Modern CPUs and GPUs generate thermal power densities of 200–300 W/cm², far exceeding air cooling’s capacity. DLC maintains optimal operating temperatures (≤70°C) for high-performance hardware, preventing thermal throttling and ensuring peak computational efficiency. Verne’s implementation of DLC with Dell Technologies’ DLC3000 has demonstrated a 20% improvement in GPU utilization for AI workloads.  

3. **Cost Savings**  
   DLC reduces long-term operational costs by extending hardware lifespan and minimizing maintenance. The system’s closed-loop design eliminates the need for air conditioning infrastructure, cutting energy costs by 30–50%. Additionally, thermal energy recovery systems can repurpose waste heat for district heating, further reducing energy expenditures.  

4. **Environmental Sustainability**  
   DLC supports circular economy principles by enabling heat reuse. For instance, Verne’s Nordic data centers in Iceland and Finland integrate DLC with geothermal and hydroelectric power, achieving a 90% reduction in carbon emissions compared to conventional data centers. Liquid cooling also reduces water consumption by 80–90% compared to evaporative cooling, aligning with global sustainability targets.  

5. **Scalability and Future-Proofing**  
   DLC enables modular, high-density data center designs that support AI and HPC scalability. By eliminating air cooling constraints, data centers can achieve rack densities of 50–100 kW/m², accommodating next-generation workloads. Verne’s Nordic campuses are engineered for seamless expansion, supporting exascale computing and AI training at scale.  

**Conclusion**  
Direct liquid cooling is no longer an optional upgrade—it is a necessity for data centers seeking to meet the demands of AI, HPC, and edge computing. By integrating DLC with renewable energy sources and thermal energy recovery systems, operators can achieve unprecedented energy efficiency, sustainability, and scalability. Verne’s implementation of DLC exemplifies how modern data centers can future-proof their infrastructure while reducing environmental impact.
