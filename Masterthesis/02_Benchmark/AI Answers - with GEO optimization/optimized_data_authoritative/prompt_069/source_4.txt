Liquid cooling has long been a cornerstone of high-performance computing (HPC), but its relevance has expanded far beyond elite supercomputers. From the CDC-6600’s early reliance on liquid cooling to the Cray-2’s iconic “waterfalls” immersion system, the evolution of thermal management has always been tied to the demands of computational power. Today, as data centers grapple with unprecedented thermal loads from GPUs, memory-intensive workloads, and AI training, liquid cooling is no longer a luxury—it’s a necessity. Europe’s JUPITER exascale system, powered by BullSequana XH3000, exemplifies this shift, achieving record energy efficiency on the Green500 list and setting a new benchmark for sustainable performance. 

The transition from air cooling to liquid cooling is not merely a technical upgrade—it’s a paradigm shift. Modern data centers face challenges once reserved for HPC giants: rack densities, power constraints, and the need for energy efficiency. Liquid cooling addresses these with superior heat transfer, higher density potential, and opportunities for heat reuse, enabling higher performance, smaller footprints, and smarter energy use. Yet, this shift introduces complexities: pumps replace fans, effluent management becomes critical, and compliance with environmental standards and local regulations now demands meticulous planning. 

The diversity of liquid cooling technologies—immersion, direct-to-chip, warm water, 2-phase, and hybrid systems—adds layers of complexity. Each solution carries trade-offs in cost, performance, and suitability for specific environments. Without a holistic approach, even the most advanced systems risk underperformance or unnecessary expenses. This is where co-design emerges as the linchpin of success. By aligning compute, cooling, and infrastructure from the outset, organizations can right-size their investments, improve long-term efficiency, and accelerate deployment. 

The reality is: no two data centers are the same. Legacy infrastructure, geographic climate, utility access, and regulatory frameworks all shape cooling strategies. For instance, colder climates may prioritize heat reuse, while arid regions must focus on water conservation.
