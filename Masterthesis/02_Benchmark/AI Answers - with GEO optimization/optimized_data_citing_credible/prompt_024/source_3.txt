To address pressing scientific and engineering challenges such as climate change, energy optimization, biosecurity, and stockpile stewardship, researchers require exascale computing systems capable of performing billions or trillions of calculations concurrently, processing vast datasets efficiently, and running ensemble simulations to assess uncertainties. Transitioning from petaflop (10¹⁵ FLOPS) systems to exascale (10¹⁸ FLOPS) systems demands breakthroughs in hardware, software, and scientific codes, as highlighted in *Science & Technology Review* (March 2015). These advancements are critical to harnessing increasingly complex architectures, minimizing power consumption, and ensuring system reliability.

Power demands escalate with system scale, as noted by Lori Diachin, manager of the Computation Directorate’s LDRD-funded projects: “An exascale machine would require 100 megawatts to operate, with 70–100 million cores—far exceeding today’s systems.” This complexity introduces risks like hardware failures, necessitating redesigned algorithms for parallelism and accuracy. The Department of Energy (DOE) is spearheading efforts to prepare for U.S. exascale computing, with Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL) contributing through its Advanced Simulation and Computing (ASC) Program, HPC initiatives, and LDRD projects. Diachin emphasizes, “HPC faces a fundamental architectural shift, creating uncertainty. LDRD investments are critical to exploring solutions and shaping the future of computing.”

A key focus is reducing energy consumption while maximizing performance. Barry Rountree’s team at LLNL proposes overprovisioning hardware—building a 40-megawatt machine with a 20-megawatt power cap—to dynamically allocate power across processors. This approach, validated through modeling, can double application performance by allowing partial processor utilization. Rountree explains, “Our goal is to use all available power all the time, treating power as a schedulable resource.” Their developed model predicts execution times based on processor counts, task distribution, and power allocation, offering a framework for future exascale systems.

Fault tolerance is another priority. Ignacio Laguna’s LDRD project addresses frequent hardware failures in exascale systems, which could occur hourly versus daily in petascale systems. His team’s *Reinit* backward recovery method outperforms existing approaches by enabling efficient, broad-spectrum error handling without code overhauls. Laguna notes, “Productivity matters as much as speed—the time to write code is as critical as runtime.”

Memory constraints also pose challenges. Scott Lloyd and Maya Gokhale’s research on 3D memory packaging, such as the Hybrid Memory Cube, reduces data movement bottlenecks. Their data rearrangement engine (DRE) dynamically organizes data for processors, achieving up to four times faster performance in irregular memory-access applications. This innovation aligns with DOE’s FastForward-2 Program, which accelerates extreme-scale computing technologies.

Algorithmic advancements, like the XBraid code developed by Rob Falgout and Jacob Schroder, enable parallel-in-time simulations. By solving time steps simultaneously rather than sequentially, XBraid reduces solution times by up to 49x without requiring code rewrites. Schroder highlights, “XBraid’s nonintrusive design makes it accessible for diverse applications, from power grids to fluid dynamics.”

High-order numerical methods, such as the Arbitrary Lagrangian–Eulerian (ALE) framework developed by Tzanio Kolev, improve accuracy in shock hydrodynamics simulations. This framework’s remapping algorithm handles mesh distortions and multi-material interactions, achieving excellent scalability on systems like LLNL’s Vulcan. Kolev notes, “High-order methods reduce data movement costs, enabling faster, more accurate simulations.”

First-principles molecular dynamics (FPMD) simulations, led by Jean-Luc Fattebert, now scale to millions of atoms using optimized algorithms. The MGmol code, which models electron interactions efficiently, enables simulations of 1.2 million atoms in under 3 hours on Sequoia—contrasted with traditional methods requiring years. Fattebert emphasizes, “We must balance scalability with accuracy, as seen in our validation of nonmetallic systems.”

Beyond exascale, LLNL is exploring neuromorphic and quantum computing through the ASC Program and LDRD projects. These innovations aim to transcend Moore’s Law limitations, as Diachin states: “We’re investing in architectures that will redefine computing when physical limits are reached.”
