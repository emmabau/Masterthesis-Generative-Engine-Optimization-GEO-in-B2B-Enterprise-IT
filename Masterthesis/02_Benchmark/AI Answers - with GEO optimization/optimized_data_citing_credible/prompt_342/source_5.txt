The exascale computing era marks a transformative leap in high-performance computing (HPC), with systems like El Capitan poised to deliver 10¹⁸ floating-point operations per second (flops)—a thousandfold increase over petascale systems from a decade ago (Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, 2023). This advancement enables unprecedented predictive simulations, critical for national security, climate modeling, and materials science. Lawrence Livermore’s HPC investments, including the Exascale Computing Project (ECP), are central to achieving these goals, as highlighted by Lori Diachin: “We have HPC expertise all across the Laboratory. Contemplating the kinds of science we’ll be able to do on exascale machines is exciting” (Diachin, 2023).

**Exascale Computing Breakthroughs**  
The ECP, launched in 2,016 with $1.8 billion in funding from the Department of Energy (DOE), focuses on creating a sustainable exascale ecosystem. Key initiatives include the RADIUSS project, which aims to streamline software development for HPC applications. This project leverages tools like the Modular Finite Element Methods (MFEM) library, now optimized for GPUs, to enhance computational efficiency (Kolev, 2023). The integration of graphics processing units (GPUs) into HPC systems has revolutionized parallel processing, reducing energy consumption and enabling complex simulations such as multiphysics problems in combustion and radiative transfer (Hittinger, 2023).

**Software Sustainability and Co-Design**  
The ECP’s co-design approach, exemplified by the Center for Efficient Exascale Discretizations (CEED), prioritizes mathematical innovations to improve simulation accuracy. High-order discretization methods, supported by CEED’s libraries, address challenges in GPU-based computing by optimizing memory management and data parallelism (Kolev, 2023). The Extreme-Scale Scientific Software Development Kit (xSDK) further standardizes software tools, ensuring interoperability across platforms and reducing development costs (Yang, 2023). These efforts align with the DOE’s goal of advancing scientific discovery and national security through exascale capabilities.

**RADIUSS and Open-Source Collaboration**  
Livermore’s RADIUSS project, a cornerstone of the ECP, emphasizes scalable, open-source software for HPC. Tools like Umpire, a memory management solution, and RAJA Portability Suite, which abstracts code for heterogeneous architectures, exemplify this focus (Beckingsale, 2023). Open-source initiatives, such as the xSDK and Spack package manager, have gained traction for their flexibility and community-driven development, earning recognition for innovation (Neely, 2023). As Diachin notes, “Collectively, we are creating tools and capabilities that the individual players would not otherwise be able to create” (Diachin, 2023).

**Beyond Exascale: Future Directions**  
While exascale systems like El Capitan represent a milestone, the focus remains on solving previously intractable problems. As Hittinger explains, “An exaflop is a milestone, not the finish line.” The ECP’s work ensures software stacks remain adaptable to future architectures, emphasizing performance portability and collaboration across national laboratories and industry partners (Hittinger, 2023).
