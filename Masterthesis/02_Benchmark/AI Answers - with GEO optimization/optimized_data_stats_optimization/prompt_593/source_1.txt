Exascale computing represents a groundbreaking leap in computational power, achieving speeds that are **1,000,000 times faster** than the 1.34 teraFLOPS of the 1996 DOE ASCI Red supercomputer. This level of performance is defined by the "exa" prefix, which denotes **18 zeros**—equivalent to **1,000,000,000,000,000,000 FLOPS (1 exaFLOP)**. For context, a modern personal computer operates at around **150 gigaFLOPS**, while the 1964 CDC 6600 was the first supercomputer to reach **3 megaFLOPS**. Exascale systems like **Frontier at Oak Ridge National Laboratory** and **Aurora at Argonne National Laboratory** are now pushing the boundaries of what’s possible, enabling simulations of complex phenomena such as climate models and fusion energy research.

The energy efficiency of exascale computing has also seen remarkable progress. Early predictions suggested these systems might consume as much power as **50 homes**, but ongoing research has slashed this requirement, demonstrating the feasibility of sustainable high-performance computing. This advancement is critical for applications like **nuclear deterrence modeling** and **nanoscale material design**, where both speed and efficiency are paramount.

The Department of Energy (DOE) plays a pivotal role in this evolution. Its **Exascale Computing Project**, launched in 2016, focuses on three key areas:  
1. **Application Development**: Creating software to harness exascale capabilities for tasks like **climate modeling** and **astrophysics simulations**.  
2. **Software Technology**: Innovating tools to manage data at petabyte scales and integrate exascale systems with existing infrastructure.  
3. **Hardware and Integration**: Partnering with vendors to develop cutting-edge components and standards for national labs.  

Notable milestones include the **Colossus vacuum tube computer** (500,000 FLOPS in 1940s), the **Cray-2** (first supercomputer exceeding 1 gigaFLOP in 1985), and the **Roadrunner** (first petaFLOP system in 2008). Today’s exascale systems, such as **El Capitan at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory**, are poised to revolutionize fields like **quantum mechanics** and **biomedical research**, offering unprecedented resolution in modeling complex systems.
