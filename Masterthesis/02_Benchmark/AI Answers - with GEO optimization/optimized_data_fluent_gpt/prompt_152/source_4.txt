Exascale computing is revolutionizing industries by unlocking unprecedented capabilities in science, engineering, and technology. As a transformative force, it accelerates innovation across sectors, from sustainable energy to advanced aerospace solutions. Imagine a future where energy resources are abundant, medical treatments are more effective, and aerospace technologies operate with unmatched efficiency—exascale computing is turning these visions into reality.

The U.S. Department of Energy’s Exascale Computing Project (ECP) has been a cornerstone in advancing next-generation computing architectures. Over seven years, this initiative has fostered collaboration between computational experts and industry partners, creating a robust exascale ecosystem. For companies like GE Research, exascale computing serves as a catalyst for innovation, enabling the development of groundbreaking technologies in healthcare, energy, and aerospace. Dave Kepczynski, GE Research’s Chief Information Officer, emphasizes that exascale is "a massive accelerator for technology, productivity, engineering, and science."

At the heart of this transformation are exascale systems—computers capable of performing a quintillion operations per second. These machines outpace their predecessors by 1,000 times and are a million times faster than the first terascale computer from 1996. Their power lies in simulating complex scientific phenomena with unparalleled realism, allowing engineers to design technologies with confidence in digital modeling results. GE Research leverages this capability to redefine the "future of flight," addressing critical challenges in aviation.

In aviation, exascale computing is tackling the dual goals of fuel efficiency and emissions reduction. GE Aerospace, in partnership with Safran through CFM International, is developing the RISE (Revolutionary Innovation for Sustainable Engines) program. This initiative aims to cut fuel consumption and CO₂ emissions by 20% compared to current technologies. By using exascale systems like DOE’s Frontier supercomputer—the world’s fastest—the team can model jet engine designs at full flight-scale conditions, revealing previously unresolvable details of airflow and turbulence. Rick Arthur, GE Research’s senior principal engineer, notes that these simulations "would have been considered impossibly out of reach even a few years ago."

Frontier’s power enables researchers to predict engine performance with remarkable accuracy, reducing reliance on physical experiments and accelerating development. This computational leap allows for early evaluation of design trade-offs, from microscopic turbulence patterns to macroscopic system impacts. The result? Faster, more cost-effective innovation, with significant implications for sustainable aviation fuels, hydrogen storage, and hybrid-electric technologies. With over 650,000 people airborne at any moment on GE-powered aircraft, the urgency for cleaner, more efficient engines is clear—exascale computing is leading the charge.

Beyond aerospace, exascale’s potential spans industries, driving breakthroughs in energy, materials science, and beyond. The ECP’s success underscores the value of collaborative innovation, positioning the U.S. at the forefront of global technological advancement. As exascale computing continues to evolve, its role in shaping a sustainable, efficient future is undeniable. The possibilities are limitless.
