Liquid cooling is a transformative approach for reducing energy consumption in data center cooling systems. This document provides an in-depth survey of liquid cooling technologies, their implementation strategies, and insights into developing standardized specifications for liquid-cooled racks. The goal is to highlight how these technologies can optimize energy efficiency, reduce infrastructure costs, and adapt to diverse operational environments.

**Overview**  
Liquid cooling in data centers encompasses a range of technologies, from indirect methods like computer room air handlers (CRAHs) to direct immersion cooling. The key advantage of transferring heat close to the source lies in the ability to use warmer cooling fluids, which significantly improves energy efficiency. This is driven by advancements in chiller performance and the potential for free cooling.  

Most liquid cooling solutions are hybrid systems, combining liquid cooling with traditional air cooling. While these systems often incur higher upfront costs compared to air-cooled equipment, the long-term energy savings and capital efficiency can offset these expenses. The decision to adopt liquid cooling depends on factors such as:  
- Reducing overall data center energy consumption (IT power + infrastructure)  
- Cooling high-density equipment  
- Limited capacity in existing air-cooled facilities  
- Availability of chilled water (CHW) plant or cooling tower capacity  
- Space for new cooling infrastructure  
- Willingness to invest in purpose-built servers  

**Key Technologies**  
1. **CRAH (Computer Room Air Handlers)**  
   CRAH systems transfer heat from air to a building’s cooling water system. A retrofit project at a Digital Realty Trust data center demonstrated an 8% annual energy savings through improved CRAH controls and fan efficiency.  

2. **Overhead Cooling Units**  
   These units hang above equipment racks, pulling hot air from hot aisles, cooling it via refrigerant heat exchangers, and redistributing cold air to cold aisles. Refrigerant’s non-conductive properties and phase-change heat removal capacity make this system ideal for space-constrained environments.  

3. **In-Row Cooling**  
   InRow systems (Schneider Electric) extract heat from hot aisles, cool it with chilled water or refrigerant, and return it to cold aisles. A dual heat exchanger design in a Schneider Electric prototype showed significant energy efficiency gains when chilled water and cooling towers are nearby.  

4. **Enclosed Cabinets**  
   Racks and servers are fully enclosed with air-to-water heat exchangers. This minimizes heat leakage to the room and allows cooling water usage to scale with server load. High reliability is critical due to the intense heat loads involved.  

5. **Rear-Door Heat Exchangers (RDHx)**  
   RDHx devices, resembling radiators, are placed at server rack exhaust points. Hot air is cooled by circulating cold water from chillers or cooling towers, reducing rack outlet temperatures and enabling room-neutral cooling. A LBNL study highlighted lessons from a RDHx demonstration project.  

6. **Conduction Cooling**  
   Thermal blocks (heat risers) transfer heat from components to a microchannel heat exchanger, cooled by water or refrigerant. The "Chill-Off 2" demonstration showed conduction cooling outperformed other methods, followed by RDHx systems.  

7. **CPU Coldplates**  
   Coldplates with microchannel water circulation replace traditional heat sinks. A LBNL study found that Cisco servers with Asetek cold plates captured 50-60% of IT heat under varying conditions, demonstrating their adaptability.  

8. **Immersion Cooling**  
   Electronics are submerged in dielectric fluids like 3M Novec 649, enabling efficient cooling without compressors. Two-phase immersion cooling, where fluids boil to absorb heat, offers exceptional efficiency but faces challenges with engineered fluids.  

**Featured Resources**  
- **Liquid Cooled Standards Initiative**: Berkeley Lab and industry partners are developing a specification for liquid-cooled racks, covering fluid properties, pressure, temperature, and system design to support scalable, vendor-neutral solutions.  
- **NREL Thermosyphon Hybrid System**: This project reduced water usage by 50% in NREL’s HPC data center, saving 4,400 m³ of water annually. It combines thermosyphon cooling with existing systems to minimize water consumption while maintaining energy efficiency.  

By leveraging these technologies and standards, data centers can achieve significant energy savings, reduce environmental impact, and adapt to evolving computational demands.
